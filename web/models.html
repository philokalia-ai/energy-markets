Model lines

Beyond the physics: the pink and yellow lines

The solid blue line on every chart is the physics counterfactual — a competitive re-clearing of the European day-ahead market from fundamentals (fleets, fuels, forecasts, the JAO flow-based network). No price is ever a regression target: the bidding form's named characteristics (about 40 — scarcity onsets, water values, the tranche ladder) were chosen with price error in view on calibration windows and checked on held-out ones, and the code flags every one of them as price-informed. Its job is to be a defensible counterfactual, not the best point forecast. The dashed lines are its honest statistical companions. This page explains what they are, what they are good for, and what they can never do.

The three models

linemodelwhat it isfitted on prices?
Physics (solid blue) The Euphemia-style competitive clearing of all 39 zones from ex-ante fundamentals. Not regressed on prices. Its ~40 named characteristics were calibrated on price error and validated out-of-sample.
Physics + ex-ante GBM (dashed pink) The physics price plus a per-zone gradient-boosted model of the residual (settled − physics), trained only on features known before the 12:00 CET gate: the order book's demand, RES/import shares and margin, D-2 fuel closes, calendar. It is trained on the cv37 physics line, so it is drawn only on days that have a cv37 physics base — not on days produced by the current model. Yes — on the residual.
Pure-stats GBM (dashed yellow) No physics at all: a per-zone gradient-boosted model of the settled price itself, from lagged settled prices (24 h / 48 h / 168 h, weekly mean), calendar, fuels, and the published D-1 load & RES forecasts. The strongest honest "just use statistics" baseline. Yes — directly.
Timestral TSFM (dashed violet, shaded q10–q90) A pretrained time-series foundation model (Mistral's Timestral-209M), used zero-shot, forecasting tomorrow's hourly price. It is issued twice on D-1, both before the 12:00 CET gate: at 06:30 UTC from the settled price history alone, and at 10:40 UTC with the frozen lead-1 physics forecast added as a known-future input. Its 21 quantiles also give P(price ≤ 0) per hour; averaged 50/50 with a small D-1 logistic model of collapse days (refit on settled ≤ 0 hours), the per-day maximum is the negative-price risk shown as a badge when it reaches 30 %. Not fine-tuned on our data — but it reads settled prices (and, at 10:40, the physics forecast), and the risk blend's logistic is trained on settled prices. A prediction, never part of the counterfactual.

Timestral backtest (Sep–Nov 2026, 39 zones, 54k hours, lead 1): MAE 28.0 €/MWh from prices only (06:30), 25.1 with the physics forecast as input (10:40); P(≤ 0) blend Brier 0.009, AUC 0.97. It extrapolates statistically, so — like the yellow line — it lags regime breaks and forecasts collapses mostly where recent history shows them. Timestral weights are used under their Modified-MIT licence.

Who wins where

On the cross-validated table above, the hybrid (pink) wins where the physics is strongest — the continental core (DE/FR/BE/NL/AT/CH/CZ: MAE 13.2 vs stats 13.5), Poland–Slovakia–Hungary, the Baltics — and ties in the Nordics. The stats line (yellow) wins where the physics has a known structural gap — Italy (9.7 vs 12.7) and Iberia — because a lag model isn't dragged by the model's bias shape. Physics keeps the best spike precision everywhere north of the Alps (0.84–1.00): when the blue line calls an extreme hour, it is calling real, mechanical scarcity — not pattern-matching. Under the strict rolling-origin test the hybrid beats physics in all four quarters only in IT-NORTH, IT-CNORTH, IT-CSOUTH, IT-SOUTH, IT-Calabria, SE1 and SE2.

What the dashed lines can never do

  • Measure conduct. The stats model learns the market's actual bidding behaviour — including any exercise of market power — into its lags; it can only ever agree with the market. The physics line is the competitive benchmark the market is compared against: the persistent gap between blue and settled in tight evening hours (Hungary, Poland, the Baltics) is a finding, not an error.
  • Handle a regime they never saw. A fuel shock, a new interconnector, a war outage move the physics line correctly on day one; a lag model needs weeks of new history.
  • Explain themselves. Every blue-line price decomposes into the order book on the book page. The dashed lines are predictions, not explanations.

Honesty notes

  • The cross-validated table uses 5-fold cross-validation grouped by day, but the folds shuffle days rather than respecting time order, and the published pink line's frozen model was trained on the whole window — so its 14.9 is flattering. The strict rolling-origin test above is the honest comparison. The physics line is never regressed on prices and uses only ex-ante inputs, but its calibrated characteristics were chosen on windows inside this same period, so its number is not a strict hold-out either.
  • All pink-line features are gate-legal (nothing after D-1 12:00 CET); the yellow line's lagged prices are auction results published up to D-2 12:45 CET — also gate-legal.
  • Where a day has no dashed lines, the models had no validated series for it — gaps are shown, never interpolated.

Scenario counterfactuals and the model lines

The scenario case studies (the data-center, cold-ironing and bring-your-own-power exercises) take their delta from physics alone: a scenario compares two competitive clearings by the same engine, and only the physics engine can clear a world that never happened. Where a scenario is shown as a forecast (tomorrow's price with the data center connected, say), it is the physics-delta composite: the Timestral base forecast plus Euphemia's Δ (scenario − base) for each zone-hour. It is never Timestral's own response to the change — a statistical model reacts to a structural shift unreliably (0.1× to 1.9× the physics Δ in our tests). The case-study figures are labelled with the model version that produced them; a rerun on the current model (cv44) is in progress.
