Methodology

A competitive counterfactual of the European power market

What would electricity prices be if every participant behaved competitively? We simulate the European day-ahead market from physical fundamentals alone, publish the predictions before the market clears, and score them honestly as reality settles. The gaps that persist are not model errors — they are measurements of how the real market deviates from competition.

One distinction runs through everything below, so we make it first and plainly: we fit the inputs, but we never fit the prices. Wind, solar and load are predictions — openly statistical machine-learning models, scored like any forecast. Prices are not predicted at all: they are mechanistically constructed from the physical generation fleet, short-run fuel costs and the coupled transmission network. No price parameter is ever tuned against the price errors we report.

The two layers — what we fit, what we construct

The model is built in two layers with a hard wall between them. Getting this distinction right is the whole epistemological claim of the project, so here it is side by side.

Layer 1 · fitted

The inputs — predictions

Wind, solar and load are things we predict, and we are completely open that this is statistics. Per zone and target we train LightGBM models (keeping the older linear packs where they still win the head-to-head), and score them like any forecast.

  • Trained on GFS previous_day1 weather vintages — the value a run issued on D−1 predicted for each delivery hour. Applied identically at train and serve time, so there is no lookahead and no train/serve skew (the vintage discipline is a headline honesty feature, not a footnote).
  • RES targets are capacity-normalized (a ratio against the trailing-30-day p95), so a growing fleet does not drift the forecast; each (zone, target) ships whichever model — new LightGBM or committed linear pack — won the out-of-sample scorecard.
  • Default-ON in the ex-ante track for the five pilot zones (GR, ES, DE_LU, SE2, NL); the 39-zone rollout is in progress.

Layer 2 · constructed

The prices — never fitted

Prices are not a regression target. The clearing engine builds each zone's order book mechanistically and solves the same coupled auction the real market solves. Every parameter is a nameable market characteristic, justified by physics or public data — never a knob turned to reduce a price error.

  • Unit-level supply ladders at short-run marginal cost (real TTF gas, daily EUA carbon, fuel emission factors); reservoir hydro at its opportunity cost — a water value tied to dryness and drawdown versus the seasonal norm.
  • Regime-gated behaviours that exist in the market, gated ex-ante and kill-switchable — e.g. the solar-surplus price-taker floor (cv31), which only activates when the day-ahead solar share of load is high, and is inert everywhere else.
  • Cross-border ATC coupling on 39 zones with two-pass opportunity anchors; zonal prices are the duals of the balance constraints, exactly as in the real coupling.
  • No parameter is tuned against price errors on the evaluation sets; activation gates are preregistered and held out (Set A to calibrate, Set B scored once).

What this is

This site tracks an ex-ante simulation of the European day-ahead electricity market — 39 bidding zones cleared jointly, the way the real EUPHEMIA algorithm clears them, with cross-border transmission constraints. The model ingests only what a market participant could know the evening before delivery: ENTSO-E load and wind/solar forecasts, offered transfer capacities, fuel and carbon prices, reservoir levels, and the physical generation fleet. From these it builds supply and demand curves per zone, couples them over the transmission network, and solves for the market-clearing price of every hour.

The sharp claim is not "no parameters" — the book has plenty, and each is a nameable market characteristic (a zone's gas premium, a hydro system's water value). It is that no parameter is fitted against the prices the model is scored on: the methodology is transparent, every input is strictly ex-ante, and activation gates are validated out-of-sample on a held-out window rather than tuned in-sample. A model fitted to prices would absorb exactly the competitive deviations we exist to measure.

Why — the research thesis

A competitive market should price at the marginal cost of the marginal unit. Our simulation is that competitive benchmark, reconstructed bottom-up. Where the real market persistently clears above the counterfactual — in specific zones, seasons, or hours — that residual is a finding about market behavior: scarcity signals, strategic bidding, or the exercise of market power. This is why we refuse to fit the model to prices: a fitted model would absorb exactly the deviations we are trying to measure.

How the model works

  1. Fleet and costs. Every dispatchable generator in each zone, with marginal costs built from real TTF gas futures, daily EUA carbon prices, and fuel emission factors — short-run marginal cost, no bid markup.
  2. The order book. Thermal units bid their marginal cost; renewables bid the D-1 forecast as price-takers; reservoir hydro bids its opportunity cost (a water value tied to gas prices, reservoir dryness and seasonal drawdown); demand is the D-1 load forecast, nearly inelastic at the cap.
  3. The coupled clear. All 39 zones clear simultaneously under cross-border capacity limits (the ex-ante flow rule uses flow climatology — never same-day observations). Zonal prices are the duals of the zonal balance constraints, exactly as in the real market coupling.
  4. Two passes. Hydro- and nuclear-anchored zones re-bid against the first pass's coupled price, so opportunity costs are consistent across the footprint.
  5. Honest inputs only. Everything is D-1-legal. When an input is not published in time, the day is skipped — never backfilled from hindsight.

The two tracks, stated plainly — the record vs the product

The two layers above give the model two distinct jobs, and we keep their scoreboards separate on purpose. It is important not to confuse them:

  • The record measures the MECHANISM. The counterfactual backfill — the multi-year history at the current code version (cv31) — runs on the reference inputs: ENTSO-E's published D-1 load and wind/solar forecasts, the same information set the market itself cleared on. Holding the inputs fixed to the market's own isolates one question — given those inputs, where would competitive prices sit? — so the residual is a clean measurement of the clearing mechanism and of conduct. Our fitted weather predictions never enter the record.
  • The ex-ante track measures the PRODUCT. The live forecast runs on our own fitted weather inputs (Layer 1) and freezes before the auction gate, so every row is provable ex-ante by its timestamp. This is the deployable forecast a participant could act on the evening before delivery; it is scored separately and becomes the headline as it accumulates.

The reference track (what the historical scoreboard shows today) freezes in the evening — before delivery, but after the auction results are public — and answers "given the market's own inputs, where would competitive prices sit?". The ex-ante track answers the harder question "what will the market do tomorrow?" from weather alone. Same clearing engine, different inputs, two honestly-labelled scoreboards.

The ex-ante guarantees

Every prediction on this site satisfies, by construction:

  • Frozen before delivery. An hour-level guard makes it impossible to write a value whose delivery hour has passed. Predictions are never revised; the record is append-only.
  • Provenance per row. Every prediction records the timestamp it was made, so anyone can verify what was known when.
  • Pre-auction where possible. The morning run freezes predictions before the day-ahead auction gate closes (12:00 CET) — ex-ante with respect to the market clearing itself, not just to physical delivery.
  • Complete market days only. A delivery day is a Europe/Athens market day (the 24-hour window starting 21:00/22:00 UTC the evening before, DST-aware); a day is published only when all 24 hours can be predicted.

The daily schedule

  • 00:00 UTCData fill: actuals for settled days; realized days are scored and the scoreboard updates.
  • 08:00 UTCMorning window — fill + forecast before the auction gate (10:00/11:00 UTC). Rows written here are pre-auction. On days ENTSO-E inputs have not yet propagated, the run honestly writes nothing.
  • 17:30 UTCEvening fill — ENTSO-E's D-1 items propagate to the bulk export late for large zones (~19:00 UTC for Germany and Italy), so the evening run fills whatever the morning could not see. Writes never overwrite a morning row.

The 7-day horizon and the vintage record

The Next 7 days view publishes an hourly price forecast for every upcoming market day. Because the decisive ENTSO-E inputs exist only for tomorrow, the leads are honest about their information:

  • D−1 is the full model forecast on complete published inputs — frozen the evening before delivery.
  • D−2 … D−7 are the weekly-persistence baseline: the frozen model forecast from the same weekday one week earlier — a deliberately simple bar to beat. Now that the weather layer has shipped, these leads are being converted to true re-forecasts on fresh multi-day weather vintages, pilot zones first, with the per-lead scoreboard showing the improvement against exactly this baseline as the rollout reaches each zone.

Every day the pipeline writes the newly-due vintage for each future day and never overwrites an earlier one — so for every delivery day D the record holds what we said at D−7, D−6, …, D−1, each stamped with the moment it was made. The "What we said, when" panel shows exactly that: all vintages overlaid, oldest faintest, against the settled actual once the day realizes. Forecast revisions are data, not embarrassments — they are how the model's information gain per day of lead time is measured.

Bidding strategies — how each region's order book is built

Every zone's hourly supply and demand curves are constructed from fundamentals, with a published bidding methodology: where a parameter is calibrated it is a market characteristic — a zone's gas premium, a hydro system's water value — fitted on ex-ante data and validated out-of-sample, never a markup tuned to the prices scored against. The common machinery:

  • Thermal plants bid short-run marginal cost: gas plants at TTF futures / efficiency + CO₂ (daily EUA price × emission factor) + O&M; coal and lignite from their own fuel and carbon stacks. No strategic markup — that gap is what we measure, not what we assume.
  • Must-run behavior: the cheapest thermal units (within ~15% of gas cost, up to the day's residual peak) offer the deepest part of their minimum load nearly free — mimicking plants that will not cycle off for a few cheap hours.
  • Wind and solar are price-takers: the forecast volume is offered at €1/MWh (reference track: ENTSO-E's D-1 forecast; ex-ante track: our weather-model prediction).
  • Demand is the D-1 load forecast, ~98% inelastic at the price cap with a small elastic tail — day-ahead demand barely responds to price.
  • Imports/exports enter through the coupled clearing's transmission constraints; where a zone structurally leans on imports, the available import capacity is credited against its scarcity margin.
  • Scarcity uplift: when the day's supply margin over peak demand is thin, offers steepen — the competitive scarcity signal, not a calibration to prices.

On top of that, each region has a profile reflecting how its market actually sets prices:

  • Hydro-dominated zones (Norway, Alpine CH/AT, Southeast Europe) — reservoir hydro does not bid its (near-zero) running cost but its opportunity cost: a water value tied to gas prices, scaled down when reservoirs are wet and up when dry (weekly reservoir levels vs the seasonal norm; Sweden's north additionally follows the seasonal drawdown cycle).
  • Two-pass opportunity anchors — in hydro-anchored zones (Norway, CH, AT, BE, SK) and nuclear-anchored France, flexible fleets re-bid in a second pass against the first pass's coupled price: a Norwegian reservoir or a French nuclear plant asks "what is my energy worth to the whole connected market?", not just locally.
  • Continental thermal zones (DE/LU, NL, PL, CZ) and the Baltics — the fleet is trued to registry installed capacity (ENTSO-E's per-unit output history undercounts these fleets); the Baltics additionally credit their import interconnectors (Estlink, NordBalt) in the scarcity margin, since thin domestic fleets ride the Nordic system.
  • Italy's six zones — a dedicated profile for the peninsula's structurally tighter, gas-set market with its own scarcity parameters.
  • Crisis honesty — where a fuel type's real availability sits far below nameplate (e.g. lignite fleets in decline), capacity is derated to its recent 95th-percentile output, so the book reflects the plants that actually run.

The full parameterization is one ZoneProfile per zone in src/merit_order/zone_profiles.jl — every choice justified by physics or public data; calibrated parameters are market characteristics, validated on held-out windows rather than fitted in-sample.

Strategy that is not in the table

Being honest about the limits of the view above: some of what makes two zones behave differently does not live in the profile at all. These do:

    Scoring, honestly

    As days settle, predictions are scored per zone and per lead time against the official day-ahead auction prices: MAE (mean absolute error, €/MWh), bias (mean simulated − actual — negative means the model under-prices, which for a competitive counterfactual is a signal, not a bug), and correlation of hourly prices. Whole-sample, never cherry-picked. On the current record (cv31, all 39 zones, pooled hourly), correlation rises year over year with the solar buildout: 0.634 in 2025 (MAE €26.5) and 0.669 in 2026 (MAE €30.3). Weighting each zone by its traded volume, zones at correlation ≥ 0.75 cover 63.7% of that volume in 2025 and 61.5% in 2026; Germany reaches 0.86 and Greece about 0.85. These figures come from a committed scoring artifact (cv31-record-scores.md). Weak zones (the Nordic hydro north, some small zones) are shown with the same prominence as strong ones.

    The cv31 record activated a regime-gated price-taker floor for the six continental solar zones (DE_LU, FR, PL, BE, CZ, CH): in deep-solar hours their order book can now genuinely clear below zero, so the model catches midday price collapses it used to miss. Measured on the floor zones, collapse-hour detection (sim ≤ €5 when the settled price is ≤ €5) rises from 14.8% to 18.2% while the false-alarm rate barely moves (0.07% → 0.10%) — +391 collapse middays caught for +47 false alarms across ~160k hours. Because the gate is ex-ante and regime-scoped, all-hours aggregates barely move by design and no zone is harmed outside the regime.

    The clearing solver itself is validated against real published auctions — fed the actual GME (Italian) and OMIE (Iberian) day-ahead bid books it reproduces the official zonal price exactly wherever the published book alone determines it (max |Δ| = €0.00 across the well-determined GME cells): reproduce it →.

    The weather layer — shipped, and independent of late inputs

    ENTSO-E's day-ahead load and renewables forecasts publish late and only for the next day, which caps the reference track at lead 1. The weather layer removes that ceiling by predicting wind, solar and load ourselves from raw weather — and it has shipped: per-zone LightGBM models on GFS previous_day1 vintages, default-ON in the ex-ante track for the five pilot zones (GR/ES/DE_LU/SE2/NL), with the 39-zone rollout underway. Wind uses an OpenStreetMap extraction of 115,390 turbines across the footprint; solar uses GFS irradiance, a clearness index and sun geometry; load uses temperature, irradiance and degree-hours over population-weighted cities.

    The point of predicting the inputs is not tidier forecasts — it is the collapse question. Near the point where predicted wind+solar approaches load, a small solar-forecast error flips whether the midday price collapses to ≤ €5 (or below zero), and that classification dominates continuous error there. The committed input scorecard measures exactly this: against the broken linear pack, the ML solar restores Greek collapse-hour detection from 8/16 to 13/16 midday hours — matching the reference track's own 14/16 — because the capacity-normalized model sees the crashes the pack's clear-sky under-prediction hid. And where the committed pack was outright broken, the ML repairs it: the Dutch load model's correlation goes 0.19 → 0.86. Both numbers are from committed artifacts (wiring.md, the input-upgrade scorecard). Discipline shows in the costs too: richer solar over-collapses Greece on the one genuinely-expensive pilot day, a false alarm we report rather than hide, per the rule "gate activation on the collapse false-alarm rate, not MAE alone".

    What we tested and rejected

    Negative results are part of the record, and we keep them visible as evidence of the discipline — a mechanism ships only when it clears its preregistered gates on held-out data:

    • Inter-temporal commitment bidding (block orders with startup dynamics) was built, tested across 14 zones, and rejected — it helps only thermal-cycling zones in winter and hurts hydro-dominated ones.
    • A plant-level physical simulation of renewables was evaluated and found unnecessary; one sun-geometry feature carries the physics.
    • The Nordic reservoir-wetness water value was implemented exactly to its frozen prereg, then measured NO-SHIP (conflicted): the only arm that met the within-regime floor tripped the envelope-correlation falsifier on its own zones, so no arm passed all gates and the branch stays unmerged (results).
    • The cv30 export-capability and surplus-floor package failed three frozen gates on the calibration set — it manufactured new cap-hours and breached correlation envelopes — so it was not scored on the hold-out and did not ship (results).

    Each of these has its numbers and prereg in the repository's docs/ — the full investigation record, kept whether or not the answer was yes.
