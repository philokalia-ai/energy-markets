The boundary — how the model treats the world outside the footprint

The 39-zone footprint is not the whole electrical world: every hour power crosses its edge. The model says two different things about its neighbours, and this surface makes the difference legible and honest. Elastic book — the neighbour bids into the clear on its own fundamental cost (GB, UA). Fixed injection — a price-taker observed schedule, lagged ex-ante, that does not respond to price (TR, AL, MK, and every unnamed neighbour). No price here is fitted. ← back to the price map

The boundary ring

The footprint sits faint in the centre; each neighbour is a node at its compass bearing, each edge a modelled border to its in-footprint anchor zone. The day slider is shared with the map — edges carry the day's net flow where the coupled solve publishes it, and say so honestly where it does not.

Fixed injection vs elastic book — the teaching panel

The load-bearing distinction, drawn. A fixed injection sells its full observed quantity at any price (a vertical line). An elastic book is a stepped ladder rising from the neighbour's own cost — so the border can now go the other way when the footprint is cheaper than the neighbour.

The neighbours — one card each, the same skeleton

KIND · how it enters · anchor · capability sizing · borders · measured effect · roadmap. The elastic cards fill every field; the fixed cards make a virtue of the blanks. Every score is quoted from the ledger, never recomputed.

The ex-ante flow rule — what makes every injection honest

Every fixed injection, and the ATC-gap floors under the elastic books, rely on a flow we do not have at auction time (tomorrow's flow publishes after the gate). So the footprint defaults to the :v3 rule — the fastest admissible estimate.

How a boundary book propagates into the coupled clear

A boundary book is just orders in the border zone's book. Because it clears with everything else, its effect propagates through the two-pass anchors to zones that never touch the border — the same emergent coupling the scenario surface documents.

We fed the engine the actual published bid books of two exchanges that publish them — GME (Italy, 7 days across 4 seasons) and OMIE (the Iberian pool, 20 days) — and asked one question: given these real bids, does our solver find the same price the exchange did? On every cell where the bids alone pin a unique price, it does — exactly. This validates the clearing mechanism in isolation. It is deliberately a narrow claim: the published books are only the domestic layer of a coupled market, so on most Italian zone-hours the price is actually set by an import at the margin that isn't in the per-zone book at all (77.7% of GME cells). That gap is not a solver error — it is exactly the cross-border coupling the rest of this page is about. Everything hard lives in the inputs to the solver: the book we construct (pillar 5) and the forecasts we predict (pillars 2–4). The solver itself is exact.

Why a zonal price is a dual

A market-clearing price is not a number we choose or predict — it falls out of the solve. The auction maximises total economic surplus subject to one balance equation per zone (supply + imports = demand + exports) and one capacity limit per border. The price of a zone is the shadow price of its balance equation: the amount total surplus would rise if that zone needed one more MWh. That is why it equals the cost of the marginal unit serving the zone — and why, when a zone leans on imports, its price is set by a plant in a neighbouring zone. The price is an output of the coupled optimisation, the dual variable, not an input to it.

When every border constraint is slack, all coupled zones share one price. When a border binds — flow at its ATC limit — the two zones it joins can hold different prices, and the difference is that border's congestion rent. The next two panels show both cases.

The second pass — anchor feedback

Reservoir hydro and French nuclear don't have a fuel cost that sets their bid — they have an opportunity cost: the value of holding the water (or the nuclear output) for a better hour. But "a better hour" is defined by the coupled price, which doesn't exist until the market clears. So the footprint clears twice: the first pass produces a coupled price; the hydro and nuclear fleets re-bid their opportunity cost against that price; the second pass re-clears. The result is water values that are consistent across the whole connected system — a Norwegian reservoir and a French reactor both pricing against the same market-wide signal.

A two-pass approximation, not a full storage optimisation — the storage charge/discharge cycle is a known modelling gap, kept visible (see the model map on the About page).

    Case studies — counterfactuals on the counterfactual

    Beyond scoring, the same engine answers what-if questions. Each case study defines a scenario through the library's scenario hooks (the scenario API), re-clears one to two years of market days offline against the published data extract — a single zone or the full 39-zone footprint — and compares against the identically built baseline. The objective is always the same question: if X connects to the grid, how much more does everyone pay? The two runs differ only in the scenario, so the price delta is causal within the model — and because the model is a competitive counterfactual, these are competitive-market estimates. Full records, scripts and provenance: docs/experiments/scenario-exercises.

    1 · A 574 MW data center connects in Greece

    Objective. A hyperscale data center draws a constant 574 MW — about 9% of average Greek load — in every hour of the year. What does that do to the day-ahead price that every other consumer pays? Firm always-on demand belongs in the load series itself, so the scenario uses the load_modifier hook: the extra megawatts propagate into net demand, scarcity margins and hydro water values, exactly as real load would.

    GR · 2025-07-01 → 2026-06-30 hook: load_modifier runs: gr_scn_base vs gr_scn_dc574 364 days · 0 solver failures
    datacenter_574mw.jl — run it yourself against the public extract 
    using Euphemia, Dates

# scenario: +574 MW of firm, always-on demand in every timeslot
dc_load = (ts, load_mw) -> load_mw + 574.0

for day in Date(2025, 7, 1):Day(1):Date(2026, 6, 30)
    generate_energy_prices("GR", day;
        order_method  = :merit_order,
        save_to_db    = true,
        clearing_mode = "gr_scn_dc574",   # label the run
        load_modifier = dc_load)          # the scenario hook
end

    2 · Cold ironing: every ferry & cruise ship plugs in at berth

    Objective. All in-scope passenger ships at the 21 monitored Greek ports connect to shore power (onshore power supply) instead of running engines at berth. The demand profile is measured — AIS port-visit data with EMSA at-berth loads: mean 126 MW, strongly seasonal (~205–250 MW in cruise season, ~30 MW in winter, peak 369 MW). Ships connect regardless of the day-ahead price, so the scenario uses the extra_orders hook: new price-inelastic demand bid at the price cap — the real-data incarnation of the documented "ships request power" example.

    GR · 2024-07-01 → 2025-06-30 hook: extra_orders runs: gr_scn_base vs gr_scn_ops 365 days · 0 solver failures
    cold_ironing_ops.jl — run it yourself against the public extract 
    using Euphemia, Dates

# OPS_MW: measured hourly shore-power demand (MW), keyed "yyyymmdd-HH"
ops = ctx -> [SimpleOrder(:demand, 3000.0, OPS_MW[ts[1:11]],
                  Symbol(ctx.zone), DateTime(ts, dateformat"yyyymmdd-HHMM"),
                  ctx.resolution_minutes)
              for ts in ctx.timeslots if get(OPS_MW, ts[1:11], 0.0) > 0]

for day in Date(2024, 7, 1):Day(1):Date(2025, 6, 30)
    generate_energy_prices("GR", day;
        order_method  = :merit_order,
        save_to_db    = true,
        clearing_mode = "gr_scn_ops",   # label the run
        extra_orders  = ops)            # price-inelastic bids at the cap
end

    3 · The same data center, with Europe in the loop

    Objective. The single-zone runs above hold net imports at historical values — an upper bound for Greece, and silent about neighbors. Here the data-center scenario re-clears on the full 39-zone coupled footprint (enriched cross-border network, two-pass opportunity anchors): imports respond to the new demand, and every neighboring zone re-prices. 1,825 39-zone market days solved overnight against the public extract (~200 days/hour on two solver workers, zero failures).

    4 · Cold ironing, all of Europe at once

    Objective. Cases 2–3 electrified Greek ports. Here every in-scope passenger ship at 294 TEN-T ports across 22 countries plugs into shore power — the EU AFIR + FuelEU Maritime at-berth obligation, priced on the coupled market. Demand is built from 3.57 million real AIS port-call dwell events over two years, binned to EMSA at-berth loads and mapped to each bidding zone (Italy → 7 zones, Sweden → 4, Denmark → 2).     About these numbers

    This is a no-fit competitive counterfactual — a fundamentals-based market-clearing model, not a statistical forecaster. Predictions are frozen before delivery, never revised, and scored honestly as days settle. Be precise about what today's scores are: the reference track uses ENTSO-E's published D-1 forecasts and freezes in the evening — before delivery, but after the auction. The truly ex-ante track — our own weather-based wind/solar, frozen before the auction gate — is being wired in and will be scored separately. Nothing is fitted to the outcomes it is scored against, so persistent gaps between simulated and actual prices are findings about how the real market deviates from competitive behavior — not model errors to be tuned away.
