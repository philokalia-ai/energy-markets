The boundary — how the model treats the world outside the footprint

The 39-zone footprint is not the whole electrical world: every hour power crosses its edge. The model says two different things about its neighbours, and this surface makes the difference legible and honest. Elastic book — the neighbour bids into the clear on its own fundamental cost (GB, UA). Fixed injection — a price-taker observed schedule, lagged ex-ante, that does not respond to price (TR, AL, MK, and every unnamed neighbour). ← back to the price map

The ex-ante flow rule — what makes every injection honest

Every fixed injection, and the ATC-gap floors under the elastic books, rely on a flow we do not have at auction time (tomorrow's flow publishes after the gate). So the footprint defaults to the :v3 rule — the fastest admissible estimate.

How a boundary book propagates into the coupled clear

A boundary book is just orders in the border zone's book. Because it clears with everything else, its effect propagates through the two-pass anchors to zones that never touch the border — the same emergent coupling the scenario surface documents.

We fed the engine the actual published bid books of two exchanges that publish them — GME (Italy, 7 days across 4 seasons) and OMIE (the Iberian pool, 20 days) — and asked one question: given these real bids, does our solver find the same price the exchange did? On every cell where the bids alone pin a unique price, it does — exactly. This validates the clearing mechanism in isolation. It is deliberately a narrow claim: the published books are only the domestic layer of a coupled market, so on most Italian zone-hours the price is actually set by an import at the margin that isn't in the per-zone book at all (77.7% of GME cells). That gap is not a solver error — it is exactly the cross-border coupling the rest of this page is about. Everything hard lives in the inputs to the solver: the book we construct (pillar 5) and the forecasts we predict (pillars 2–4). The solver itself is exact.

Why a zonal price is a dual

A market-clearing price is not a number we choose or predict — it falls out of the solve. The auction maximises total economic surplus subject to one balance equation per zone (supply + imports = demand + exports) and one capacity limit per border. The price of a zone is the shadow price of its balance equation: the amount total surplus would rise if that zone needed one more MWh. That is why it equals the cost of the marginal unit serving the zone — and why, when a zone leans on imports, its price is set by a plant in a neighbouring zone. The price is an output of the coupled optimisation, the dual variable, not an input to it.

When every border constraint is slack, all coupled zones share one price. When a border binds — flow at its ATC limit — the two zones it joins can hold different prices, and the difference is that border's congestion rent. The next two panels show both cases.

The second pass — anchor feedback

Reservoir hydro and French nuclear don't have a fuel cost that sets their bid — they have an opportunity cost: the value of holding the water (or the nuclear output) for a better hour. But "a better hour" is defined by the coupled price, which doesn't exist until the market clears. So the footprint clears twice: the first pass produces a coupled price; the hydro and nuclear fleets re-bid their opportunity cost against that price; the second pass re-clears. The result is water values that are consistent across the whole connected system — a Norwegian reservoir and a French reactor both pricing against the same market-wide signal.

A two-pass approximation, not a full storage optimisation. Pumped storage is energy-limited — a daily budget from its own recent output, spread over the day by a full-day pass before the hourly clear — but pumping demand is still not bid, so the charge half of the cycle remains a known gap, kept visible (see the model map on the About page).

    How a bid is built — from named ex-ante characteristics

    Every order in the merit book carries a strategy label — the honest source-side reason that block sits where it does. This page teaches that vocabulary and wires each term to (a) the named parameter or formula that produced it, and (b) its provenance: market-observed vs declared & out-of-sample validated. No price here is hand-authored — the cost constants, tranche form and glossary are generated from the running model; drag the sliders to explore. See these blocks live in the order book →

    1 · The SRMC of a bid — the cost floor

    The short-run marginal cost is the atom of every thermal offer: fuel ÷ efficiency + carbon + O&M, with no scarcity markup. Pick a fuel; drag TTF (gas) and EUA (carbon) to feel the cost assemble.

    2 · From cost to offered blocks — tranches & must-run

    One unit's available capacity is sliced into priced blocks. The deepest must-run block is bid below cost by design; upper tranches carry the scarcity/peak markup that only bites in tight hours.

    3 · The characteristics table — parameter → bid category

    Every named characteristic, its value/source, the strategy label it produces, and a provenance badge: observed (market data, no choice) or declared (a named characteristic, chosen — usually with price error in view on a calibration window, often then checked on held-out days; never regressed on prices).

    One card per strategy label — the exact vocabulary the order-book table prints. Generated from the model's own STRATEGY_DESCRIPTIONS, so the book and this page can never disagree.

    5 · Per-zone profiles — why zones behave differently

    The 39 zones resolve to a handful of distinct parameter vectors. Pick a zone to see only the fields that differ from the SEE base, each with its provenance badge — plus the honest “strategy not in the table” caveats.

    6 · Calibration honesty — constructed, calibrated, never regressed

    Pillar 5 is constructed: its inputs are market-observed, its form is a small set of declared parameters. Most of those were calibrated — chosen with price error in view on a calibration window, many then checked on held-out days inside the same period — and the code flags each one as price-informed.     About these numbers

    Timestral + physics is the headline forecast: the Timestral time-series model, given the settled prices up to the day before and our frozen lead-1 physics clear as a known-future input (rMAE 0.578 against the D-1 naive over a two-year backtest of this configuration). It reads settled prices, so it is a prediction, not part of the counterfactual. The physics clear is a constructed competitive counterfactual: a market-clearing model in which no price is ever a regression target. Its bidding form has about 40 named characteristics calibrated with price error in view, each published and flagged. Its forecasts are frozen before the 12:00 CET auction gate and never revised, so persistent gaps between simulated and settled prices are findings about how the real market deviates from competition, not errors to tune away.
